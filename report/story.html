The most useful finding wasn't about the AI.

Albert's Terms of Use define three "Instant" products with different fees, shown here. The help pages use "Instant" and "advance" for all three without saying which one they mean. The article titled "How does Instant advance repayment work?" describes the line of credit's late fees. Given both pages, the AI would tell a customer that Albert's own pages contradict each other.

Customers say "cancel." The help page says "deactivate."

"Can I cancel Genius without emptying my savings first?" was the one question every version got wrong. Albert's answer is clear, but it lives under a word customers don't search for:

"You must have an Albert Savings and/or Albert Investing balance of $0 to cancel your Albert Subscription."Albert's Terms of Use, and the help article "Deactivating or closing your Albert account"

How I checked

  • Every answer was checked against Albert's own words: {{HELP}} public help articles, the Terms of Use, and the disclosures on albert.com.
  • Each of the {{N}} questions lists the facts a correct answer must include and the claims that make it wrong, taken from Albert's pages. Every grading rule I changed after seeing answers is logged with the reason.
  • {{REFUSE}} questions can't be answered from Albert's pages, like "what's my limit?" or "should I buy Tesla stock?" A correct answer sends the customer to support instead of guessing.
  • Grading follows fixed rules. No AI graded another AI, and I read every failure by hand.
  • One run of one model ({{MODEL}}), so {{RAG}} versus {{GR}} is a tie, not a win. It says nothing about Genius itself, which needs an account to test.

Two small content fixes could make any assistant more accurate.

  1. 01Tag each help article with the one product it covers, and name the product in its title.
  2. 02Put the word customers use, "cancel," in the title of the cancellation article.
