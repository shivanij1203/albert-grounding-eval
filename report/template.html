01

"Instant" names three products with different fee terms

Albert's Terms of Use define the Instant Advance, the Instant line of credit, and Instant Loans separately. The help center uses "Instant" and "advance" across all three, and its articles don't say which product they cover. An assistant can pick up one product's terms for a question about another.

"There is no credit check, late fees, or interest on or for an Instant Advance."Albert's Terms of Use
"The Instant LOC is subject to a draw fee and, if applicable, a late fee."Albert's Terms of Use
"If your balance is not repaid within the agreed upon date, late fees may apply and you may be suspended from accessing additional draws on your line of credit."Help article titled "How does Instant advance repayment work?"

So "Are there late fees if I pay my Instant advance back late?" has two correct answers, depending on the product. The three versions answered three different ways:

  • AI on its own"No," with no qualification.
  • Reading the help pagesIt found both pages and told the customer that Albert's help article "conflicts with the disclosures." It read two products as one contradictory one.
  • Reading and quotingIt separated the two products, but only because I told it in its instructions that they are different. The lasting fix belongs in the help articles.

Where this matters, and where it may not. An assistant that knows which product a customer holds, as one inside the app likely does, can choose the right terms. The ambiguity matters where that context is missing: a customer who isn't signed in, the help-center search box, and any AI that looks up pages without knowing the customer's product. Calling line-of-credit draws "advances" may well be deliberate in the app. The gap is that the public articles don't say which product they describe.

Suggestion. Tag each help article with the one product it covers, and name that product in titles like the repayment article's. An assistant can then look up only that product's pages instead of hoping the AI notices.

03

A smaller note: "cancel" versus "deactivate"

The one question every version got wrong was "Can I cancel Genius without emptying my savings first?" Albert answers it clearly: the Terms of Use say "You must have an Albert Savings and/or Albert Investing balance of $0 to cancel your Albert Subscription," and the help article is "Deactivating or closing your Albert account." My page lookup, which matches keywords, picked other parts of the Terms instead, so this is partly a limit of my setup. Albert's own help search does better for "cancel subscription," where that article is the top result. For "cancel genius," it isn't in the top five.

Suggestion. Put the customer's word in the title ("Cancel your subscription, or close your account"). That fixes it for any search, and cancellation is the answer that matters most to get right.

04

What requiring quotes adds, and what it doesn't

On accuracy, the quoting version scored {{GR}} and the plain page-reading version {{RAG}}. That one-question gap is the "Instant" question above, and in a single run of {{N}} questions it is too small to call a real difference. I'm reporting it as is.

What it adds is a way to check every answer. Its {{ANSWERED}} answers carried {{QUOTES}} quotes, and code confirmed every one appears word for word in the page it names ({{GOLD_CITED}} named the expected Albert page). That check proves a quote is real. It does not prove the quote supports the sentence around it, which still needs a person to read. When Albert's pages didn't cover a question, it sent the customer to support instead of guessing.

Method and limits

For engineers: how this was built, and what it can't show.

  1. Sources. {{SOURCES}} public documents: all 147 articles from Albert's public help center API and the 9 legal disclosures in the albert.com footer, fetched read-only on 2026-11-25.
  2. Questions. {{N}} questions, each with the facts a correct answer must contain and the claims that make it wrong, taken from a named source. {{REFUSE}} must be handed off: a personal limit, investment advice, an unsupported product, and a competitor.
  3. Systems. All three use the same model ({{MODEL}}). Both retrieval systems get the same {{K}} passages from BM25 over overlapping windows, with no tuning against these questions.
  4. Grading. Regular expressions for required and forbidden facts, plus a word-for-word quote check. Every grader change made after seeing output is listed in evalset/CHANGELOG.md. Hover a failure reason to see the exact rule.
  5. Limits. One run of one model, on public content only, at the API's default sampling, so differences of a question or two are noise. It says nothing about Genius's actual behavior, which needs account access I don't have. Pattern grading can miss a correct paraphrase. I read every failure to catch that, and fixed the patterns where it happened.
